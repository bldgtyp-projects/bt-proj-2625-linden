The drawings show a fireplace in the Living Room (sheets A5.2 and A7.1), noted as "code compliant fireplace," with
the type not yet selected. While we understand the aesthetic and lifestyle benefits of a fireplace, any
combustion appliance inside an airtight Passive House needs careful selection and coordination with the ventilation
system. The type of fireplace chosen now sets how much of that work is needed.

#### What the Boulder code allows

- **Wood:** The <VarLink hrefKey="narrative.energy_code.code_base_url" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><Var
  k="narrative.energy_code.name" /></VarLink> permits only EPA Phase II or Colorado Phase III certified
  wood-burning devices (R402.4.2).
  A traditional open masonry fireplace does not meet this requirement.
- **Air barrier and insulation:** Exterior walls behind the fireplace must be insulated to the same level as the
  adjacent walls, with an air barrier separating the fireplace from the insulation (Table R402.4.1.1).

#### Our recommendation

**The simplest option is an electric fireplace.** It has no flue, no combustion air and no carbon monoxide risk,
needs no special coordination with the ventilation system, and raises no issues for Passive House certification.
Current electric units with water-vapor "flame" effects come much closer to the look of a real fire than earlier
products.

If a wood fire is important to the owners, use a **closed, DIBt-approved room-sealed wood stove**, never an open
hearth. Open
fireplaces cool the house through air leakage whether in use or not, and contribute to poor indoor air quality with a
higher risk of unhealthy carbon monoxide, NOx and particulate levels, which can aggravate respiratory problems for
occupants.

#### Passive House requirements for wood stoves

PHI's certification criteria set the following conditions for any wood-burning appliance inside a certified building.

- **Room-sealed only.** The stove must draw all of its combustion air from outdoors through a dedicated, sealed duct,
  and operate independently of the indoor air.
- **Carbon monoxide detectors** in the room with the stove and nearby rooms.
- **A safety device that shuts down the fans on underpressure.** A differential-pressure switch compares the stove
  room with outdoors. If the room goes negative, it shuts down the ERV and, ideally, the range hood, so they cannot
  pull combustion gases out of the stove and into the house.

#### Use a DIBt-approved stove

Many stoves and inserts sold as "outside-air ready" are not room-sealed in the sense PHI requires. The clearest proof
that a stove is room-sealed is an approval from the German Institute for Building Technology (DIBt). DIBt tests a
stove for room-air-independent operation: the sealed combustion chamber and a self-closing, tightly sealing door are
pressure-tested and the leakage measured, to show the stove keeps its flue gases in when a ventilation system changes
the pressure in the room. Approved models receive a DIBt approval number (Z-43.12-xxx), and some manufacturers mark
them "RLU" (raumluftunabhängig, room-air independent).

**We recommend selecting a DIBt-approved, room-air-independent stove.** When choosing a unit, confirm:

- The DIBt approval number for the exact model and configuration, and that the approval is current. Approvals expire
  and must be renewed by the manufacturer.
- That the model is also EPA certified, as the Boulder code requires. Many DIBt-approved stoves are European models,
  so check the [EPA certified wood heater database](https://cfpub.epa.gov/oarweb/woodstove/index.cfm?fuseaction=app.search)
  and the US distributor before specifying.
- Installation instructions showing the sealed outdoor-air connection as **mandatory**, not optional.

Many popular models, including the Stûv 21, 30 and 16-H, treat the outdoor-air connection as optional and carry no
DIBt approval.

Use the smallest firebox that meets the design goals. Because the house needs so little heat, even the smallest unit
available will be oversized, and occupants may find they need to open a window during use to avoid overheating the
room.

#### Coordination with the ERV

The model assumes a <Var k="narrative.mechanical.erv.manufacturer_name" />
<VarLink hrefKey="narrative.mechanical.erv.link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><Var
k="narrative.mechanical.erv.type_name" /></VarLink>. If a wood stove is used:

- Set parameter 1.5 "Imbalance permissible" to **No** and 1.6 "Imbalance (open fireplace)" to **0%**, so the
  unit always runs balanced. Confirm with Brink that this also disables the frost-protection imbalance.
- Connect the differential-pressure switch to the unit's built-in **"fire automation"** input (connector X14), which
  stops both fans.
- Wire the switch through a relay so that a wiring fault or a dead switch also stops the fans (fail-safe). As built,
  the input only acts when the contact closes.
- The unit's X19 output can drive a relay to cut the range hood at the same time.

Differential-pressure switches suitable for this use are generally available with a trip point of about 20 Pa.
The mechanical engineer should select the switch, trip point and reset method.

#### Commissioning and testing

At the blower-door test, have the technician also measure the pressure in the stove room with the range hood, ERV and
all other exhaust devices at their highest settings and the fire lit. This follows the German "4 Pa test" (DVGW G 625)
that chimney inspectors use. The room should stay within 4 Pa of outdoors with the makeup air working as designed.
Also confirm that the pressure switch shuts down the fans at its trip point, including when the sensor is
disconnected.

#### Owner documentation

Collect the stove documentation, the CO detector datasheet and location plan, the pressure-switch datasheet, wiring
diagram and commissioning record, and photos of the combustion-air connection and rating plate into one booklet for
the owners. The owners should sign it to confirm that the fireplace is for occasional use only and that the
pressure switch and makeup air must never be disabled.

Good operating practice for any wood fire:

- Never leave a fire unattended. Extinguish it completely before leaving the room or going to bed.
- Use only dry, seasoned wood, and keep fires small.
- Inspect and maintain the chimney regularly.
- Avoid running the range hood or other exhaust appliances while a fire is burning.
- Keep the CO detectors tested and working.
