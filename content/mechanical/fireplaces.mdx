The drawings show a fireplace in the Living Room (sheets A5.2 and A7.1), noted as "code compliant fireplace," with
the type not yet selected. While we understand the aesthetic and lifestyle benefits of a fireplace, any
combustion appliance inside an airtight Passive House needs careful selection and coordination with the ventilation
system. The type of fireplace chosen now sets how much of that work is needed.

#### What the Boulder code allows

- **Wood:** The <VarLink hrefKey="narrative.energy_code.code_base_url" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><Var
  k="narrative.energy_code.name" /></VarLink> permits only EPA Phase II or Colorado Phase III certified
  wood-burning devices (R402.4.2).
  A traditional open masonry fireplace does not meet this requirement.
- **Air barrier and insulation:** Exterior walls behind the fireplace must be insulated to the same level as the
  adjacent walls, with an air barrier separating the fireplace from the insulation (Table R402.4.1.1).

#### Our recommendation

**The simplest option is an electric fireplace.** It has no flue, no combustion air and no carbon monoxide risk,
needs no special coordination with the ventilation system, and raises no issues for Phius certification.
Current electric units with water-vapor "flame" effects come much closer to the look of a real fire than earlier
products.

If a wood fire is important to the owners, use a **closed, room-sealed wood stove** with an airtight firebox and
sealed outdoor combustion air, never an open hearth. Open
fireplaces cool the house through air leakage whether in use or not, and contribute to poor indoor air quality with a
higher risk of unhealthy carbon monoxide, NOx and particulate levels, which can aggravate respiratory problems for
occupants.

#### Phius requirements for fireplaces and wood stoves

Phius sets the following conditions for fireplaces in a certified building:

- **No open, unvented, or ethanol fireplaces.** Natural-draft (open) fireplaces, unvented fireplaces, and ethanol
  fireplaces are not permitted.
- **No gas fireplaces under Phius ZERO.** Phius ZERO does not allow fossil-fueled fireplaces of any kind, including
  natural gas and propane.
- **Airtight firebox with outdoor combustion air.** A wood stove or other biomass fireplace must have an airtight
  firebox, with all of its combustion air brought from outdoors through a dedicated, sealed duct.
- **Counted in the energy model.** Phius treats a wood stove as a heat source. By default it is modeled as supplying
  25% of the home's heating at 60% efficiency, and we read this as applying to a sealed wood-burning fireplace or
  insert as well. Every variant in this report includes it. If an electric fireplace is chosen, we will take it out
  of the model.
- **Carbon monoxide detectors** placed so that they can be heard throughout the house. Phius also recommends
  depressurization sensors, since a failed ventilation fan can pull the house negative and draw combustion gases
  into the home even with a sealed stove.

If a gas fireplace is wanted, the project would need to pursue **Phius CORE** rather than Phius ZERO. Phius CORE
allows a gas fireplace, provided an electrical circuit sized for an equivalent electric unit is run to within 6 ft of
it so that it can be replaced later. See [Appliances and Venting](#appliances-and-venting) for the gas appliance
restrictions that also apply.

We also recommend the following safety measures for a wood stove in an airtight home:

- **A self-closing, tightly sealing firebox door**, with the stove's installation instructions showing the sealed
  outdoor-air connection as mandatory, not optional.
- **A sealed combustion-air duct and flue.** The combustion-air duct should be sealed to ductwork air-tightness class
  C or D, with a flue connector without longitudinal seams, tightly connected to the stove and chimney.
- **A safety device that shuts down the fans on underpressure.** A differential-pressure switch compares the stove
  room with outdoors. If the room goes negative, it shuts down the ERVs and, ideally, the range hood, so they cannot
  pull combustion gases out of the stove and into the house.

#### Choosing a stove

Many stoves and inserts sold as "outside-air ready" do not have the airtight firebox that Phius requires. When choosing
a unit, confirm:

- That the manufacturer documents the stove as room-sealed (room-air independent), with a sealed firebox and door,
  and with the combustion air drawn only through the outdoor-air connection.
- That the model is EPA certified, as the Boulder code requires. Many room-sealed stoves are European models, so
  check the [EPA certified wood heater database](https://cfpub.epa.gov/oarweb/woodstove/index.cfm?fuseaction=app.search)
  and the US distributor before specifying.
- Installation instructions showing the sealed outdoor-air connection as **mandatory**, not optional.

Use the smallest firebox that meets the design goals. Because the house needs so little heat, even the smallest unit
available will be oversized, and occupants may find they need to open a window during use to avoid overheating the
room.

#### Coordination with the ERV

If a woodstove is used, the ventilation controls should be designed so that:

- The ERVs always run balanced. Any imbalance settings, including those used for frost protection, should be
  disabled or confirmed with the manufacturer not to depressurize the house. The added frost-protection preheaters
  recommended in [Ventilation System Balancing](#ventilation-system-balancing) help make this possible.
- The differential-pressure switch stops the supply and exhaust fans of both ERVs when it trips.
- The switch is wired fail-safe, so that a wiring fault or a dead switch also stops the fans.
- The range hood is shut off at the same time.


#### Commissioning and testing

Whether or not certification is pursued, the ventilation system must be commissioned and tested to ensure safe operation. 
Plan a stove-room pressure test for the same site visit as the final blower-door test. It is a separate test, done
with the blower door off: the technician measures the pressure in the stove room relative to outdoors with the fire
lit and the range hood, ERVs and all other exhaust devices at their highest settings. This follows the German "4 Pa test" (DVGW G 625)
that chimney inspectors use. The room should stay within 4 Pa of outdoors with the pressure switch (and any makeup
air) working as designed. The 4 Pa limit is conservative, and does not rely on the stove's own airtightness.
Also confirm that the pressure switch shuts down the fans at its trip point, including when the sensor is
disconnected.

A step-by-step field procedure, including the test sequence, data log and photo checklist, is available here:
[Wood Stove Field Pressure Test Procedure (PDF)](/assets/mechanical/wood-stove-field-pressure-test-procedure.pdf) 
. This test should be repeated periodically as part of ongoing building maintenance.

#### Owner documentation

Collect the stove documentation, the CO detector datasheet and location plan, the pressure-switch datasheet, wiring
diagram and commissioning record, and photos of the combustion-air connection and rating plate into one booklet for
the owners. The owners should sign it to confirm that the fireplace is for occasional use only and that the
pressure switch (and makeup air, if installed) must never be disabled.

Good operating practice for any wood fire:

- Never leave a fire unattended. Extinguish it completely before leaving the room or going to bed.
- Use only dry, seasoned wood, and keep fires small.
- Inspect and maintain the chimney regularly.
- Avoid running the range hood or other exhaust appliances while a fire is burning.
- Keep the CO detectors tested and working.
