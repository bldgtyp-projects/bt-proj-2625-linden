This report covers the design-phase energy modeling for the new single-family home in Boulder, Colorado. The energy
model was built from the drawings and models received on
<Var k="source_files.cad_files_received_date" />, and carries about 5,880 ft² of treated floor area inside about
19,300 ft² of thermal envelope.

We tested five _variants_, from a Boulder energy code baseline up to a Passive House Institute (PHI) Low Energy
Building. **We recommend the PHI Low Energy Building variant**; the other four show what each step contributes. The
findings below drive the recommendations in the pages that follow.

#### What the results show

1. **The as-drawn insulation is a good start, but not enough on its own.** The insulation shown in the current drawings
   cuts purchased energy by about a fifth compared with the code baseline (from about 49,900 to 39,400 kWh/yr).

2. **Airtightness and the ERV are low-cost, high-value steps.** Tightening the building from 3.0 to 1.0 ACH50 and
   upgrading to a high-efficiency ERV cut the heating demand by about another 9,700 kWh/yr. Neither has high material
   costs, but both need careful detailing and must be written into the construction documents.

3. **The windows are the biggest remaining lever.** Moving from code-minimum to Passive House-grade triple-pane
   windows cuts the heating demand by about 14,300 kWh/yr, the largest single step after the insulation. The
   best-in-class windows in the recommended variant also keep the glass warm enough for comfort without the need for heating below
   the windows. See [Window Thermal Comfort](/windows/#window-thermal-comfort).

4. **The PHI Low Energy Building target is achievable, with a small margin.** With better windows, more insulation at
   the basement and flat roof, and 0.6 ACH50, the recommended variant reaches a heating demand of about 9.2 kBtu/ft² per
   year against a limit of 9.5. Compared with the code baseline, it uses about 60% less energy (about 19,100 kWh/yr)
   and cuts the peak heating load from about 119 to 41 kBtu/h (about 3.4 tons), a size a small residential heat pump
   system can handle easily.

5. **Summer solar gain needs attention.** Cooling demand is within the PHI limit, but the peak cooling load is above
   the recommended level in every variant, driven by sun on the east, west, and upper south glazing. See
   [Summer Radiation](/windows/#summer-radiation).

#### What we recommend

- Use the **PHI Low Energy Building** variant as the basis of design and pricing, whether pursuing certification or not. See
  [Passive House Certifications](/energy_model/#passive-house-certifications).
- Specify **best-in-class triple-pane windows** at an installed U-value of about 0.14 Btu/hr·ft²·°F, with
  non-metallic flashings and carefully developed installation details. See
  [Window Installation](/windows/#window-installation).
- Increase the **basement wall and slab insulation** to about R-30 and the **flat roof** to about R-76, as shown in
  the [Recommended Assemblies](/building_envelope/#recommended-assemblies).
- Target **0.6 ACH50** for whole-building airtightness
  and test mid-construction while the air barrier is still accessible. See
  [Building Airtightness](/building_envelope/#building-airtightness).
- Plan a **dedicated ERV** with its own ducting (about 230 cfm normal, 300 cfm boost). See [Mechanical](/mechanical/).
- Add **exterior shading** to the east, west, and upper south glazing, and consider a lower solar heat gain glass on
  the west facade.
- Use a **heat pump water heater** with a compact plumbing layout. See [Hot Water](/hot-water/).
- If a wood fire is wanted, use a **DIBt-approved room-sealed stove** with a pressure safety switch; otherwise, an
  electric fireplace is the simplest option. See [Fireplaces](/mechanical/#fireplaces).
- Because the heating margin is small, **check design changes against the energy model**, particularly changes to
  the windows, shading, insulation, or airtightness.
