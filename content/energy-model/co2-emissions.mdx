Carbon dioxide and other types of pollution which result from energy consumption are mainly responsible for the
increased warming of the earth's atmosphere and water. In order to reduce the risk of global climate change it is
important to reduce all CO<sub>2e</sub> (CO<sub>2</sub> Equivalent) emissions related to the buildings, industry and transportation across all
sectors. While there is much debate about the specific targets these reductions should achieve, one useful method
suggests that by 2030 each individual will need to meet an annual 'Carbon Budget' of roughly 2.3 tons-CO<sub>2e</sub> per person
for all activities. This would mean that an average individual's annual carbon emissions might include approximately 1
ton-CO<sub>2e</sub> / year related to their housing and building inhabitation, 1 ton-CO<sub>2e</sub> / year for their transportation, and
another 0.3 tons-CO<sub>2e</sub> / year for food. For reference, a single US-to-Europe round trip flight currently releases
approximately 4 tons of warming gases into the atmosphere. This 1 ton/person target for building emissions gives us a
useful benchmark for this building's annual CO<sub>2e</sub> emissions. Given an average annual occupancy of 
approximately <Var k="building.total_num_occupants" /> people, this building should ideally see a total annual 
CO<sub>2e</sub> emissions footprint of less than about <Var k="narrative.co2.target_tons" /> tons-CO<sub>2e</sub> / year.

Based on the modeled source energy and fuel types for the various energy uses of the building we can approximate the
average annual CO<sub>2e</sub> emissions which will result. CO<sub>2e</sub> emission totals shown below are those which result from fuel usage
by the building for heating, cooling, hot-water and all other plug-loads. The total amount of CO<sub>2e</sub> emitted as a result
of each use-type depends on both the amount of fuel used as well as the type of fuel (gas, electricity, etc.). Although
fuel-fired heating systems are permitted by Code, for this evaluation we have modeled all variants with electric-powered
heat-pump systems only.

Output Emission Rates used are from the <strong><Var k="narrative.co2.epa_subgrid_name" /></strong> Subregion. For more information on these factors see 
the [EPA eGRID Data Explorer](https://www.epa.gov/egrid/data-explorer). Source Energy Factors for all fuel types are taken
from the [EPA EnergyStar Portfolio Manager Technical Reference (2023)](https://www.energystar.gov/buildings/tools-and-resources/portfolio-manager-technical-reference-source-energy).

The recommended PHI Low Energy Building variant is estimated at about 9.9 tons-CO<sub>2e</sub> / year, about 60% less
than the Code-Minimum variant (about 25.9 tons-CO<sub>2e</sub> / year), but still well above the target of about
<Var k="narrative.co2.target_tons" /> tons. Heating remains the largest source, at about 5.7 tons-CO<sub>2e</sub> / year.
On-site solar PV, which is not included in this model, would further reduce the building's net emissions.

These relatively high emissions are due in large part to Colorado's electricity grid rather than the building itself.
The grid in this region (EPA's WECC Rockies subregion) still relies heavily on coal: in 2023, coal was its largest
single source of generation at about 32%, roughly double the US average of 16%, and the region has no nuclear
generation. As a result, each kWh of electricity used here emits about 35% more CO<sub>2e</sub> than the US average
(about 1,040 vs. 770 lb-CO<sub>2e</sub> / MWh, [EPA eGRID2023](https://www.epa.gov/egrid/summary-data)). As the
region's remaining coal plants are retired, the building's emissions will fall without any changes to the house.
